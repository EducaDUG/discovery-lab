    Life Processes & Organs

    What does it mean to be alive, and which organs keep you going?

    Real-world link Doctors can transplant organs to save lives, and scientists are building artificial organs. Your lungs let you breathe and your heart keeps you alive every second.

    The 8 life processes (MRS C GREN): tap a card to flip it

    Something is alive only if it carries out all of these. Moving, growing or being warm is not enough on its own.

    Exam trap: photosynthesis is NOT one of the eight

    Photosynthesis is how a plant makes its food, which is part of Nutrition. Respiration is not breathing: it is the chemical process in cells that releases energy from food. Sort these ideas:

    Organs: a group of tissues doing one job

    Human organs
    • Heart: pumps blood around the body
    • Lungs: take in oxygen from the air
    • Brain: controls thoughts and body functions (and body temperature)
    • Liver: removes toxins from the blood
    • Stomach: digests food
    Plant organs
    • Roots: absorb water from the soil
    • Leaves: carry out photosynthesis

    Plants do have organs. Roots and leaves are each made of several tissues working together.

    Cells & Organ Systems

    Cells make tissues, tissues make organs, organs make systems.

    In BOTH plant and animal cells
    • Nucleus: controls the cell
    • Cell membrane: controls what enters and exits
    • Cytoplasm: where chemical reactions happen
    • Mitochondria: release energy (respiration)
    ONLY in plant cells
    • Cell wall: strengthens and supports the cell
    • Chloroplasts: use sunlight for photosynthesis
    • Large vacuole: stores water and nutrients, helps shape

    Kidney function and failure

    Key idea Kidneys filter the blood, removing waste (like urea) and extra water. The waste is sent to the bladder as urine. If the kidneys fail, waste builds up in the blood, and the person may need dialysis or a transplant.

    Mixtures & Solutions

    Substances that are mixed but not chemically joined.

    Mixture

    Two or more substances not chemically joined. A mixture can be separated physically. A compound has chemical bonds, a mixture does not.

    Examples: salt and sand, oil and water, air.

    Solution

    A mixture where one substance dissolves in another. Solute = the substance that dissolves. Solvent = the substance doing the dissolving.

    Salt in water: salt is the solute, water is the solvent.

    Solute, solvent, solution

    ExampleSoluteSolventSolution
    Sugar in teaSugarWater (tea)Sweet tea
    SaltwaterSaltWaterSalt solution
    Lemonade powder in waterPowderWaterLemonade
    Ink in alcoholDyeAlcoholInk mixture
    Copper sulfate in waterCopper sulfateWaterBlue solution

    The solute seems to disappear, but it is still there, and you can get it back. A cloudy mixture has suspended solid in it (it did not dissolve).

    Evaporation & Distillation

    Getting the solute back, or getting both back.

    Evaporation

    Liquid particles gain heat energy and change into a gas. Used to separate a dissolved solid from a liquid, for example salt from water.

    Solute kept. Solvent lost as vapour.

    Distillation

    Separates a liquid from a solution.

    1. The liquid is heated and evaporates.
    2. The vapour cools and condenses into a separate container.

    Keeps both the solute and the solvent.

    Real-world link Astronauts on the International Space Station use distillation to recycle urine into drinking water. On Earth, distillation is used to purify water.

    Chromatography

    Separating the colours hidden inside an ink.

    How it works
    • Chromatography separates a mixture of coloured substances (dyes).
    • Stationary phase = the paper.
    • Mobile phase = the solvent that moves up the paper.
    • The finished paper is a chromatogram.
    Why the colours separate

    Different dyes have different solubility. A dye that is more soluble dissolves in the solvent and travels further up the paper. A less soluble dye is left behind.

    Black ink is not one dye. It contains multiple dyes.

    Real-world link Forensic scientists use chromatography to match inks and dyes from evidence, and food scientists use it to check food colourings.
