    Meet the Microbes

    Tiny living things that live everywhere.

    What are micro-organisms?
    • Tiny organisms, usually seen with a microscope.
    • They live in air, water, soil and inside your body.
    • Not all of them cause disease: many are helpful.
    • Your body has more bacterial cells than human cells.
    Three main types
    • Bacteria: single-celled, prokaryotic (no nucleus).
    • Viruses: much smaller; they only reproduce inside a living host cell.
    • Microscopic fungi: yeast and moulds.
    FeatureBacteriaVirusesMicroscopic fungi
    SizeTiny (one cell)Smallest: about 500 million fit on a pin headSmall (yeast is one cell; moulds are thread-like)
    Cells?One prokaryotic cell, no nucleusNo cells at allCells with a nucleus
    ReproduceDivide by themselvesOnly inside a host cellSpores or budding
    ExamplesSalmonella, RhizobiumInfluenza, chickenpox, COVID-19Yeast, mould, Penicillium

    Match the microbe to its description

    Which type of micro-organism?

    Fact A teaspoon of soil can hold more bacteria than there are people on Earth, and one bacterium can become 8 million in less than 24 hours.

    Antibiotic resistance: put the story in order

    How resistance happens
    • Antibiotics kill most bacteria, but a few have chance changes that let them survive.
    • The survivors multiply and pass the change on.
    • Soon most of the bacteria are resistant and the medicine stops working.
    How to help
    • Only take antibiotics when a doctor says so.
    • Finish the whole course.
    • Never share your prescription.
    • Wash hands to stop germs spreading.

    Useful Microbes

    Yeast, bacteria and fermentation: microbes that work for us.

    In food
    • Yeast (a fungus) eats sugar and makes carbon dioxide, so bread dough rises.
    • Bacteria turn milk into yogurt and certain cheeses (they use up lactose).
    • Fermentation: microbes break down sugars. It makes kimchi, sauerkraut and soy sauce.
    In medicine and your body
    • Penicillium mould led to penicillin, the first antibiotic (Alexander Fleming).
    • Helpful bacteria in your gut help digest food.
    • Yeast balloon experiment: yeast + sugar + warm water makes gas that inflates the balloon.

    Match the microbe to its use

    Helpful or harmful?

    Yeast balloon: what is happening?

    Fact Yeast has been used to make bread and beer for over 5,000 years. Fermentation was one of the earliest ways of preserving food.

    3

    Harmful Microbes and Food Hygiene

    Disease, food poisoning and how to stop microbes spreading.

    Bacteria and viruses that make us ill
    • Bacteria: Salmonella (food poisoning), Staphylococcus (skin infections), strep throat.
    • Viruses: influenza, the common cold, chickenpox, COVID-19.
    • Food poisoning: vomiting, diarrhoea, fever.
    Stopping the spread
    • Wash hands with soap for at least 20 seconds.
    • Cook food thoroughly.
    • Keep raw and cooked food separate (use different cutting boards).
    • Refrigerate food: cold slows growth but does not kill all bacteria.

    Which microbe causes which disease?

    Bacteria or virus?

    Safe or risky food habit?

    Cross-contamination is when harmful microorganisms pass from one food or surface to another, for example from raw meat to a sandwich.

    Fact Over 600 million people get sick from contaminated food each year. Soap removes bacteria far better than water alone.

    Decomposers and the Environment

    Nature's recyclers, and microbes in soil, water and extreme places.

    Decomposers
    • Bacteria and fungi break down dead plants and animals.
    • They recycle nutrients back into the soil for plants to absorb.
    • Worms are not true decomposers, but they help by breaking dead matter into small pieces.
    • Composting: food scraps + bacteria + fungi make nutrient-rich compost.
    Microbes in the environment
    • Soil: break down organic matter and release nutrients.
    • Rhizobium lives in legume roots and fixes nitrogen into a form plants can use.
    • Water treatment: bacteria break down waste and help purify water.
    • Some live in extreme places: hot springs and the deep sea.

    Match the microbe job

    Decomposer or not?

    Put the nutrient cycle in order

    Fact One gram of healthy soil can hold up to a billion bacteria. Without decomposers, Earth would be buried under dead plants and animals.

    Germination and the Life Cycle

    The life cycle

    Germination
    • Germination: a seed begins to grow into a new plant.
    • Needs water, warmth and oxygen.
    • Does not need direct sunlight to start.

    Flowers and Pollination

    Flower parts, and how pollen gets from flower to flower.

    Flower parts
    • Anther: makes pollen (male).
    • Stigma: sticky, catches pollen (female).
    • Ovary: contains ovules; becomes the fruit.
    • Petals: attract pollinators.
    Pollination
    • Pollination: pollen moves from the anther to the stigma.
    • Insects (bees, butterflies) carry pollen as they feed on nectar.
    • Wind carries pollen from grasses, wheat and trees.

    Match the flower part to its job

    Insect-pollinated or wind-pollinated?

    PollenSticky, spiky, less of itLight and smooth, huge amounts
    StigmaSticky, inside the flowerFeathery, outside the flower
    ExamplesSunflower, roseWheat, grasses, maize
    Fact The biggest flower in the world, Rafflesia arnoldii, grows up to 1 metre wide and smells like rotting meat to attract flies.

    Fertilisation and Seed Dispersal

    From pollen to seeds, then off to new places.

    Fertilisation
    • After pollination, the male and female cells unite: this is fertilisation.
    • It forms a seed.
    • The ovary usually develops into the fruit, which protects the seeds.
    Why disperse seeds?
    • Less competition for light, water and nutrients.
    • Plants can colonise new areas.
    • It helps the species survive.

    The Heart, Blood and Vessels

    The body's transport system.

    The circulatory system
    • Moves oxygen, nutrients and waste around the body.
    • Four parts: heart, arteries, veins and blood (plus capillaries).
    • The heart has four chambers: two atria (top) and two ventricles (bottom).
    • Right side: deoxygenated blood to the lungs. Left side: oxygenated blood to the body.
    Blood
    • Red blood cells carry oxygen.
    • White blood cells fight infection.
    • Platelets help blood clot.
    • Plasma is the liquid part.

    Match the blood part to its job

    Artery, vein or capillary?

    VesselDirectionWallSpecial feature
    ArteryAway from the heartThick, strongWithstands high pressure
    VeinTowards the heartThinnerValves stop backflow

    Lungs, Pulse and Exercise

    Gas exchange, breathing, respiration and the pulse.

    Gas exchange
    • Air reaches tiny air sacs called alveoli.
    • Oxygen enters the blood; carbon dioxide leaves it.
    • The heart pumps oxygen-rich blood round the body; cells use it in respiration.
    • Breathing moves air; respiration releases energy in cells.
    Pulse and exercise
    • Pulse: the beat felt in an artery when the heart pumps.
    • Count beats in 15 seconds and multiply by 4.
    • During exercise, pulse and breathing rates rise to give muscles more oxygen.
    • Athletes often have a lower resting heart rate.

    Match the term

    Breathing or respiration?
