Level 1: The Basics (Foundational Principles)

Core concepts, Earth's four spheres, trophic energy transfer, and ecosystem services.

1. What Is Environmental Science?

Environmental Science is an interdisciplinary, problem-solving science focused on observing natural phenomena, asking testable questions, gathering empirical evidence, and designing sustainable solutions. It integrates biology, chemistry, physics, geology, hydrology, and social science.

The Water Shortage Paradox

A common misconception is that Earth is "running out of water." Because water moves through a closed hydrological loop, Earth's total water volume remains constant (~1.386 billion km³). The true crisis is the global shortage of clean, safe, and accessible fresh water fit for human consumption.

Real-World Case: In Manila, Philippines, single-use plastic sachets used for daily household goods take minutes to consume but persist in urban waterways and ocean gyres for centuries.

2. Earth's Interconnected Spheres

Earth functions as a dynamic super-system. An event in one sphere triggers ripple effects across the other three.

Sphere Primary Composition Core Function
Geosphere Rocks, minerals, tectonic plates, soils Provides physical structure & nutrient storage
Hydrosphere Oceans, ice caps, groundwater, vapor Regulates climate & transports nutrients
Atmosphere Nitrogen (78%), Oxygen (21%), Argon, GHGs Filters UV radiation & traps surface heat
Biosphere All living organisms across biomes Converts solar energy into biological tissue
Cascade Example: Undersea earthquake in Sumatra, Indonesia (Geosphere) $\rightarrow$ Tsunami wave surge (Hydrosphere) $\rightarrow$ Devastated coastal mangroves (Biosphere) $\rightarrow$ Reduced coastal carbon sequestration (Atmosphere).

3. Energy Flow & The 10% Rule

In food webs, arrows show the direction of energy transfer ($\text{Eaten} \rightarrow \text{Eater}$).

$\text{Energy at Level } N = \text{Energy at Level } (N-1) \times 0.10$

The 10% Rule: Roughly 90% of energy is lost at each trophic step as low-grade metabolic heat, respiration, and unabsorbed waste. Only ~10% is stored as biomass.

Australian Wetland Case: Benthic Algae ($50,000 \text{ kJ}$) $\rightarrow$ Tadpole ($5,000 \text{ kJ}$) $\rightarrow$ Small Native Fish ($500 \text{ kJ}$) $\rightarrow$ White-faced Heron ($50 \text{ kJ}$). Apex predators require vast territorial ranges because available energy drops exponentially.

4. Biodiversity & Ecosystem Services

Genetic DNA variations within a species
Species Variety of distinct organisms
Ecosystem Habitats across landscapes

The Four Categories of Ecosystem Services:

  • Provisioning: Raw material goods (timber, fresh water, wild food).
  • Regulating: Climate moderation, flood control in Mekong Delta.
  • Supporting: Soil formation, primary production, nutrient cycling.
  • Cultural: Eco-tourism in Kenya's Maasai Mara, spiritual sites.
Catskill Watershed Case: New York City protected its natural forest watershed rather than building a $6B filtration plant—using natural regulating services to purify drinking water.

5. Population Ecology & Limiting Factors

A population is a group of individuals of the same species in a specific, named area. Population change is dictated by:

$\Delta N = (\text{Births} + \text{Immigration}) - (\text{Deaths} + \text{Emigration})$
Exponential Growth (J-Curve) Unbounded acceleration under ideal conditions with unlimited resources ($\frac{dN}{dt} = rN$).
Logistic Growth (S-Curve) Decelerates and levels off at Carrying Capacity ($K$) due to environmental resistance ($\frac{dN}{dt} = rN \left(\frac{K-N}{K}\right)$).
Density-Dependent: Impact strengthens as density rises (disease, food competition, waste).
Density-Independent: Impact occurs regardless of density (wildfires, floods, tsunamis).

7. Land Use & Agroforestry

Industrial monoculture yields high immediate crop output but strips soil nutrients, creates edge-effect vulnerabilities, and decimates habitat diversity.

Agroforestry Advantage:

Interplanting shade trees with crops improves root architecture, boosts water retention, locks atmospheric carbon, and provides natural pest control.

Colombia Coffee Triangle: Native shade trees above coffee bushes prevent hillside erosion, sequester carbon, and provide migratory bird habitat.

8. Invasive Species & Conservation

An organism is invasive only if it satisfies 3 conditions: (1) Non-native, (2) Spreads rapidly, and (3) Causes ecological or economic harm.

IUCN Red List Hierarchy:
Least Concern Vulnerable Endangered Critically Endangered Extinct in Wild

Key Biodiversity Areas (KBAs): Global sites essential for biodiversity. Alliance for Zero Extinction (AZE) Sites: KBAs containing the last remaining wild population of an endangered species.

9. Hydrological Scarcity & Day Zero

A water reservoir is governed by mass conservation principles. Agriculture consumes ~70% of global freshwater withdrawals.

$\Delta \text{Storage} = (\text{Precip} + \text{Inflow} + \text{Recharge}) - (\text{Evap} + \text{Extraction})$

When total extraction persistently exceeds total recharge, levels drop toward "Day Zero"—when municipal pipes are shut off.

Cape Town Case (2018): Aggressive rationing, pressure reduction, and agricultural diversions cut municipal water use by >50%, successfully averting Day Zero.

10. Energy Systems & Climate Forcing

The natural greenhouse effect keeps Earth warm (+15°C instead of -18°C). The environmental problem is the enhanced greenhouse effect from fossil fuel combustion releasing extra $CO_2$, $CH_4$, and $N_2O$.

Carbon vs. Ecological Footprint:

Carbon Footprint: Total direct and indirect GHG emissions ($CO_2 e$).
Ecological Footprint: Total biologically productive land/water area needed to produce resources and absorb waste.

Peatland Deforestation: In Sumatra & Borneo, clearing peat swamp forests for palm oil converts a carbon sink into a massive greenhouse gas source.

11. Point vs. Non-Point Pollution

Point Source Discharged from a single, discrete outlet (pipe, platform). Easy to track legally.
Ex: Montara Oil Spill (2009), Timor Sea.
Non-Point Source Diffuse runoff from wide agricultural regions. Harder to regulate.
Ex: Fertilizer runoff damaging the Great Barrier Reef.

12. Ozone Depletion vs. Climate Change

Students often confuse these two phenomena. They occur in different atmospheric layers via distinct mechanisms:

Feature Ozone Depletion Global Warming
Atmospheric Layer Stratosphere (15–35 km) Troposphere (0–12 km)
Primary Drivers CFCs, Halons ($Cl^\bullet$ radicals) $CO_2, CH_4, N_2O$
Radiation Type Ultraviolet (UV-B) filter Infrared (IR) heat trapping
Global Treaty Montreal Protocol (1987) Paris Agreement (2015)
