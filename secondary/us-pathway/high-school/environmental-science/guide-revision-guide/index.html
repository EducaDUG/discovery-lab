Chapter 1

What Is Environmental Science?

A problem-solving science: observe, question, gather evidence, design a solution.

Environmental Science studies the environment — air, water, soil, ecosystems, biodiversity, climate, pollution, natural resources, and the way humans interact with all of these systems.

Key term Environment

The air, water, living things, ecosystems, and human surroundings that all interact with each other — not just "trees, animals and plants."

Being specific: why "energy" is too broad

A common mistake is naming a broad category — "energy," "transport" — without saying what the actual action or impact is. A strong answer always names the specific activity: not "energy" but "charging devices," "heating a house," or "driving a petrol car." Before writing an example down, ask four questions: What exact action is happening? What resource is being used? What impact might result? Who or what could be affected?

Clean water, not "running out of water"

A frequent misconception is that the world will simply run out of water. It won't — water constantly moves through the water cycle (Chapter 7) and stays on the planet. The real concern is access to clean, safe water. Polluted water can harm rivers, oceans, soils, ecosystems and human health even while the total amount of water on Earth stays roughly the same.

Case file

Everyday choices, real impact

Transport, energy used to power homes and devices, and waste from packaging all carry environmental consequences. Single-use plastic is often used for a few minutes but can remain a waste problem for decades. The point of studying this is not guilt — it's awareness. Understanding an impact is the first step to reducing it.

Field Test
1. What does Environmental Science study?
The environment, including air, water, soils, ecosystems, biodiversity, climate, pollution, natural resources, and how humans interact with these systems.
2. Why is "energy" too broad as an environmental-impact example unless it's explained further?
It doesn't name the specific action or impact — a stronger answer names something like heating a house, charging devices, or driving a petrol car.
3. Why is clean water a more accurate concern than "we will run out of water"?
Water stays in the water cycle, so the real issue is whether it's clean, safe and available for people, ecosystems, rivers, oceans and soils.

Chapter 2

Ecosystems & Energy Flow

Who eats whom, and where the energy actually goes.

An ecosystem is a living "home system" where organisms interact with each other and with non-living conditions — light, water, temperature, air, soil and minerals.

Key termBiotic factor

A living part of an ecosystem — a fish, plant, frog, insect, bird, snake or bacterium.

Key termAbiotic factor

A non-living part of an ecosystem — sunlight, water, temperature, air, rocks, soil or minerals.

Abiotic conditions decide whether an organism can survive somewhere at all: a kangaroo can't live underwater, a polar bear can't live in the desert. Ecosystems are dynamic, not frozen — fires, storms and earthquakes can disturb them, and recovery happens through ecological succession. Human disturbance becomes a problem when it happens too often or too intensely for the ecosystem to recover.

The food chain: which way does the arrow point?

In a food web, an arrow always points from the organism being eaten towards the organism that eats it, because the arrow shows the direction energy travels. Get the arrow backwards and the whole diagram is scientifically wrong — even if every organism in it is correct.

This is why top predators are always rare: by the time energy reaches the top of the chain, there isn't much of it left. Decomposers — fungi and bacteria — sit outside this chain, breaking down dead material at every level and returning nutrients to the soil so the cycle can start again.

RoleWhat it doesExample
ProducerMakes its own food, usually by photosynthesisGrass, algae
Primary consumerEats producersRabbit, caterpillar
Secondary consumerEats primary consumersSnake, fox
DecomposerBreaks down dead material, recycles nutrientsFungi, bacteria
Case file

An Australian pond ecosystem

A pond includes biotic factors such as fish, frogs, insects and bacteria, and abiotic factors such as sunlight, water temperature and dissolved minerals. A simple chain: algae → tadpole → fish → heron, with decomposers recycling waste back into the mud.

Field Test
1. Which way does an arrow point in a food web, and why?
From the organism being eaten towards the organism that eats it — the arrow shows the direction energy moves.
2. What does the 10% rule explain?
Why top predators are rare — only about a tenth of the energy at one trophic level transfers to the next.
3. What is the difference between a biotic and an abiotic factor?
Biotic factors are living parts of an ecosystem; abiotic factors are non-living conditions such as sunlight, water or soil.

Chapter 3

Biodiversity I: Levels & Value

Three scales of "variety of life," and why it keeps ecosystems running.

Biodiversity is not simply "lots of living things" — it is a precise idea that operates at three distinct levels.

LevelDefinitionExample
Genetic diversityVariation in DNA within one species, not just visible differencesKoalas vary genetically even though many look similar — this gives a population a better chance of surviving disease
Species diversityThe variety of different species present in an ecosystemA rainforest has far more species diversity than a monoculture plantation
Ecosystem diversityThe variety of habitats or ecosystems across a landscapeA country with deserts, forests, coastlines and grasslands has high ecosystem diversity

A common mistake is assuming more species is automatically better. If some of the added species are invasive (Chapter 4), a higher species count can actually damage an ecosystem's balance. Genetic diversity, too, is not about how different individuals look — it's about underlying variation in DNA, which supports adaptation and long-term survival.

Biome and ecoregion — related, not identical

Key termBiome

A broad, major ecological zone — desert, grassland, rainforest.

Key termEcoregion

A more specific region within a biome, defined by its own combination of climate, land and life.

A country's ecosystem diversity is about the range and contrast of habitats it contains, not simply its size. A smaller country with varied climate, altitude and coastline can have greater ecosystem diversity than a much larger country covered mostly by one habitat type.

Why biodiversity matters: ecosystem services

CategoryWhat it meansExample
ProvisioningMaterial goods taken directly from natureFood, timber, fresh water
RegulatingNatural processes that regulate environmental conditionsPollination, flood control
SupportingUnderlying processes that let other services continueNutrient cycling, soil formation
CulturalNon-material benefits people get from natureBeauty, recreation, inspiration
Case file

The New York City watershed

New York City protects the forests, wetlands and streams in its watershed rather than relying only on filtration plants. Healthy natural land filters water before it reaches people — a regulating/supporting ecosystem service saving a city money while protecting nature.

Field Test
1. What are the three levels of biodiversity?
Genetic diversity, species diversity, and ecosystem diversity.
2. Why is more species not always better for an ecosystem?
Some added species may be invasive and can damage native food webs, habitats and ecological balance.
3. Name the four categories of ecosystem services.
Provisioning, regulating, supporting and cultural.

Chapter 4

Biodiversity II: Threats & Conservation

Invasive species, restoration, and the tools that decide where conservation happens first.

Invasive species

Key termInvasive species

An organism living outside its native range that spreads successfully and causes harm to the local ecosystem, native species, or human systems. Being non-native alone is not enough — it must also spread and cause damage.

Invasive species harm ecosystems in more than one way: some prey directly on native animals, some outcompete native plants for light and space, others damage habitats or infrastructure. Around Sydney, examples include red foxes, rabbits, common mynas, African olive and lantana. In New Zealand, introduced predators such as cats cause major damage because native wildlife evolved without them.

Conservation and restoration strategies

StrategyWhat it involvesExample
In-situ conservationProtecting species within their natural habitatNational parks, reserves
Ex-situ conservationProtecting species outside their natural habitatZoos, seed banks
Active restorationActively repairing a damaged ecosystemReplanting, removing invasives, monitoring
Case file

Scottsdale Reserve, Australia — six principles of restoration

A well-designed restoration project (1) uses a reference ecosystem as a model of "healthy," (2) matches methods to the level of degradation, (3) sets measurable goals, (4) aims for full recovery, (5) integrates science with practical management, and (6) involves local people in the work. Restoration is not just planting trees — without clear goals and monitoring, good intentions don't guarantee recovery.

The IUCN Red List

Scientists classify extinction risk using a formal system, not casual words like "rare." "Threatened" is a broad umbrella term covering three increasingly serious categories:

CategoryRisk level
VulnerableModerate risk of extinction
EndangeredHigh risk of extinction
Critically EndangeredExtremely high risk of extinction

A species' category is combined with its population trend (increasing, stable, decreasing) to build a full picture of urgency. The Javan rhino is critically endangered and depends on habitat management; the koala is vulnerable, with a decreasing population trend.

Key Biodiversity Areas and AZE sites

Key termKey Biodiversity Area (KBA)

A site identified as especially important for protecting species, habitats or ecological processes — selected using evidence, not because a place looks natural.

Key termAlliance for Zero Extinction (AZE) site

A site that may be the last or most critical refuge for one particular highly threatened species. Shown on maps as polygons with a real, mapped boundary.

These tools work together: the IUCN Red List identifies which species are at risk, Key Biodiversity Areas highlight globally important places, and AZE sites sharpen the focus onto the most critical sites for particular species. Protecting only the visible habitat boundary is not always enough — planning also needs to consider indirect areas of influence, such as an upstream river feeding a protected wetland.

Climate change, pollution and biodiversity loss

Rising temperatures can put alpine flowers and their pollinating bees out of synchrony; habitat loss threatens island species such as Hawaiian honeycreepers; more frequent wildfires damage Australian biodiversity hotspots.

Key termOcean acidification

Extra carbon dioxide from human activity dissolves into seawater, making it more acidic and harder for corals to build their calcium-carbonate structures — a chemistry change, not simply a heat change, that contributes to coral bleaching.

Field Test
1. What makes a species invasive rather than just non-native?
It is outside its native range, spreads successfully, and causes harm to local ecosystems, species or human systems.
2. Put these in order of increasing extinction risk: Endangered, Vulnerable, Critically Endangered.
Vulnerable, then Endangered, then Critically Endangered.
3. How does extra carbon dioxide end up harming coral reefs?
It dissolves into seawater, makes it more acidic, and makes it harder for corals to build their structures.

Chapter 5

Population Ecology

Why populations rise, why they level off, and what actually limits them.

Key termPopulation

Individuals of the same species living in a specific, named area at the same time. "Kangaroos in New South Wales" and "kangaroos in all of Australia" describe two different populations.

A population is not a fixed number. It changes because of four factors: births and deaths (which increase and decrease it directly), and immigration and emigration (individuals moving in or out of the area).

TermWhat it measures
Population sizeThe total number of individuals
Population densityHow crowded individuals are within a given area
Population distributionWhere individuals are found across an area

Growth patterns and carrying capacity

Key termCarrying capacity

The maximum population size an environment can support over time, limited by the food, water, space and other resources available.

Limiting factors

TypeDefinitionExamples
Density-dependentBecome stronger as the population gets more crowdedFood, water, space, disease
Density-independentAffect a population regardless of how crowded it isWildfires, floods, hurricanes
Case file

Wildfire recovery and resilience

Ecosystems are often resilient after a single wildfire — populations and vegetation recover over time. Repeated, frequent fires are far more damaging: the ecosystem doesn't have time to recover, and seed banks needed for regrowth can be destroyed before they replenish.

Field Test
1. What is the difference between exponential and logistic growth?
Exponential growth rises very quickly under ideal conditions (a J-shaped curve); logistic growth slows and levels off as it approaches carrying capacity (an S-shaped curve).
2. What does carrying capacity mean?
The maximum population size an environment can support over time, given the food, water and space available.
3. Give one density-dependent and one density-independent limiting factor.
Density-dependent: food, water, space or disease. Density-independent: a wildfire, flood or hurricane.

Chapter 6

Earth's Systems

Four systems, constantly trading water, rock, gas and life.

Earth can be understood as four interacting systems rather than four separate, unrelated topics.

Case file

A tsunami: one event, four systems

A tsunami changes the geosphere through movement of the seafloor and coastlines; it directly involves the hydrosphere through the mass movement of ocean water; it damages the biosphere through harm to people, animals, plants and habitats; and it affects the atmosphere more indirectly — damaged vegetation reduces carbon dioxide uptake and oxygen release, showing that atmospheric effects can be less immediate than the other three.

The exam-ready idea: environmental events don't stay contained within one system. Effects spread through the planet as a chain of connected consequences — always look for the knock-on effect, not just the first, most obvious one.

Field Test
1. Name Earth's four systems.
Geosphere, hydrosphere, atmosphere and biosphere.
2. Using a tsunami as an example, explain how it affects at least three of them.
It changes the geosphere through seafloor and coastline movement, moves huge amounts of water in the hydrosphere, and damages people, animals and habitats in the biosphere.
3. Why is it more accurate to treat the four systems as one connected model?
Because changes in land, water, air and living things spread through the planet as connected consequences, rather than staying in one system.

Chapter 7

The Hydrosphere & Soil

97% salt, 3% fresh — and most of that is hiding underground.

The hydrosphere is every drop of water on the planet: oceans, lakes, rivers, groundwater, and the invisible vapour in the air. About 97% of it is salt water. Only 3% is fresh — and most of that isn't in a lake or river at all. It's underground, in aquifers.

Key termOsmosis

Water moves across a partially permeable membrane from where it is more concentrated to where it is less concentrated. This is exactly why a freshwater fish placed in the sea would dehydrate, and a saltwater fish placed in a river would take on too much water.

The water cycle

LayerWhat it contains
O horizon (organic)Old leaves, fungi and insects
A horizon (topsoil)Nutrient-rich soil fed by leaching; most roots grow here
SubsoilHarder, denser, fewer nutrients
Broken rockPartially weathered parent rock
BedrockSolid, unweathered rock
Field note

Vocabulary you can write, before you can explain

It's very common to be able to write infiltration, transpiration and erosion on a diagram before being able to explain what each one means out loud. That gap is exactly what examiners test — say each definition to someone else before test day.

Field Test
1. Why can't a fish be moved between fresh water and salt water?
Osmosis — water moves across the fish's body from where it's more concentrated to where it's less concentrated, causing dehydration or overload depending on the direction.
2. Trace the water cycle from evaporation back to the ocean.
Sun evaporates water → vapour condenses into a cloud → precipitation falls → water runs off over the surface or infiltrates into an aquifer → eventually returns to the ocean.
3. What is an aquifer?
An underground layer of rock or soil that stores fresh water in the spaces between particles.

Chapter 8

Land Use & Agriculture

Reading a satellite map, and the difference between farming for a market and farming to eat.

Types of forest

Forest typeDescription
Primary forestOlder, established, less recent conversion — but can still be affected by edge effects, roads, fire or nearby agriculture
Naturally regeneratingTrees growing back after past human use, without deliberate replanting
Planted forestTrees intentionally replanted to restore an area
Plantation forestTrees grown in rows, often one species, for timber/paper
Tree crops / agroforestryTrees grown for fruit, nuts, shade, or mixed farming

An exam-ready caution: primary forest does not automatically mean "no human impact." Even old, established forest can be affected by edges, roads, rivers, settlements, logging or fire. The stronger conclusion is not "no human impact," but "less visible conversion, with impact needing more evidence before it can be judged."

Key termMonoculture

A system dominated by a single species — a eucalyptus plantation, a pine plantation, a grazing pasture. Native forest with many plant species supports far more insects, birds, fungi, reptiles, mammals and soil organisms than a monoculture does.

Key termBuffer zone

A protective strip of land placed between a human land use (a farm) and a sensitive ecosystem (primary forest), reducing pressures such as noise, animal movement, erosion, invasive species and runoff.

Case file

Agroforestry vs. a single-species plantation

Clearing native scrubland for one type of tree provides economic value at an ecological cost. Agroforestry or mixed planted forest can provide similar economic benefits while supporting more biodiversity, healthier soil, longer carbon storage, and greater resilience to fire, pests and drought than a monoculture plantation.

Industrial vs. subsistence agriculture

TypeDefinitionExample
Industrial agricultureRun mainly as a business, selling most of what is producedA commercial sheep farm selling the vast majority of its stock
Subsistence agricultureFarming mainly to feed the farming family itself, more common in developing regionsSmall-scale farming and chicken-keeping in rural areas of developing countries

The same activity — keeping animals, for example — can be either subsistence farming or simply a hobby, depending on the context and the need behind it.

Key termArable land

Land capable of being ploughed and used to grow seasonal crops — wheat, corn, vegetables — distinct from pasture (livestock) or plantation (trees in rows).

On a satellite map, land use typically falls into visible categories: forest, urban housing, pasture, plantation, grassland and arable land. Agriculture covers roughly a third of the Earth's land surface, alongside cities and infrastructure and shrinking natural ecosystems that still hold most of the planet's remaining biodiversity.

Field Test
1. What is the difference between a primary forest and a plantation forest?
A primary forest is older and established with complex ecological relationships; a plantation forest is planted and managed by people, often in rows, often growing one or a few species.
2. Why can a monoculture support less biodiversity than a native forest?
It's dominated by one species, so it provides far less food, shelter and habitat variety than a native forest with many species.
3. What is the difference between industrial and subsistence agriculture?
Industrial agriculture is run mainly as a business selling most of what is produced; subsistence agriculture is farming mainly to feed the farming family itself.

Chapter 9

Water Resource Management

How a reservoir is managed — and what "Day Zero" actually means.

Applying environmental science to a real problem uses the scientific method: observation, question, hypothesis, investigation, data collection, analysis, conclusion. A hypothesis is a properly researched prediction — not a casual guess.

Key termReservoir

A place where water is stored, often behind a dam, collecting water from rain and rivers so it can be released as needed for agriculture, industry and domestic use.

Key termDay Zero

The point at which a reservoir has no water left to supply anyone — the central risk reservoir management is designed to avoid.

Reservoir management is a genuine trade-off. Cutting water use too far to protect the reservoir can collapse agriculture and cost people their livelihoods; extracting too much risks running the reservoir dry. Of the main users — agriculture, industry and domestic use — agriculture is the single largest user of water, and the largest human driver of water scarcity.

Conservation measures

MeasureHow it helps
Drip irrigationWaters crops slowly and directly at the roots, cutting farm water use by roughly 35%
Rainwater harvestingCollects and stores rainwater (e.g. tanks) for later use instead of drawing more from the reservoir
Wetland restorationRestores vegetation that helps the landscape retain water
Case file

Cape Town's near-miss, 2018

Cape Town, South Africa, came within weeks of "Day Zero" — the point at which engineers would have shut off the municipal water supply. Over-extraction, pollution and a drying climate are why cities from Chennai to Mexico City now plan for water scarcity as a real, near-term risk rather than a distant one.

Field Test
1. What does "Day Zero" mean, and why does a reservoir manager want to avoid it?
It's the point at which a reservoir has no water left, meaning nothing is left to supply farms, homes or industry.
2. What is the largest human cause of water scarcity?
Agriculture, which uses more water than domestic or industrial use.
3. Name two conservation measures and how each saves water.
Drip irrigation waters crops directly at the roots, cutting farm water use by ~35%; rainwater harvesting collects and stores rain instead of drawing more from the reservoir.

Chapter 10

Energy & Climate

Renewable vs. non-renewable, and the difference between a footprint and a carbon footprint.

TypeDefinitionExamples
RenewableNaturally replenished on a human timescaleSolar, geothermal
Non-renewableForms over very long geological timescales, used faster than replacedCoal, petroleum, uranium

The real test for "renewable" is not whether a source is natural, but whether it can be replaced quickly enough for long-term use.

Key termGreenhouse gases

Gases such as carbon dioxide, methane, nitrous oxide and water vapour that absorb and re-emit heat energy. A natural greenhouse effect is essential — it keeps Earth warm enough for life. The problem is extra greenhouse gases from human activity trapping additional heat.

Carbon footprint vs. environmental footprint

TermScope
Carbon footprintMainly greenhouse gas emissions — e.g. from flying or driving
Environmental footprintBroader: land use, food, water, packaging, pollution, transport, housing, wildlife
Case file

Palm oil

Unsustainable palm oil production has been linked to rainforest loss and biodiversity damage in some tropical regions — a single product choice connecting land use, deforestation and biodiversity all at once.

Field Test
1. Why is solar energy classed as renewable?
The Sun keeps providing energy on a human timescale; solar panels collect it whenever there is daylight.
2. What do greenhouse gases do, and what is the actual problem with them?
They absorb and re-emit heat, keeping Earth warm enough for life naturally — the problem is extra greenhouse gases from human activity trapping more heat than usual.
3. How is an environmental footprint broader than a carbon footprint?
A carbon footprint focuses on greenhouse gas emissions; an environmental footprint also includes land, water, food, packaging, pollution, transport and housing.

Chapter 11

Pollution

One traceable source, or a thousand untraceable ones.

TypeDefinitionWhy it matters
Point sourceComes from one identifiable locationAuthorities can act directly on that single source
Non-point sourceComes from many spread-out locationsNo single source to target — usually needs a broad, region-wide response
Case file

The Montara oil spill (point source)

In 2009, a blowout at the West Atlas offshore drilling platform in the Timor Sea, north of Darwin, released an estimated 4,500–34,000 m³ of oil over 75 days, covering roughly 6,000 km² of ocean. Because it could be traced to one identifiable rig, it's classified as point source pollution. (Two common errors to avoid: it happened in the Timor Sea/Indonesian waters, not the Indian Ocean, and it was a drilling-platform blowout, not a cargo ship malfunction.)

Case file

Agricultural run-off, Great Barrier Reef (non-point source)

Land clearing and farming across Queensland send topsoil and chemicals into rivers that feed the reef, blocking sunlight and damaging coral. Because it comes from many farms across a whole region rather than one traceable source, it can't simply be "shut off" the way a single factory can.

Case file

The 2022 New South Wales floods

Flood waters disturbed acid sulfate soils, which combined with decaying vegetation to produce a toxic black sludge in the Richmond River — choking oxygen, killing fish, and spreading to the coast.

Field Test
1. What is the difference between point source and non-point source pollution?
Point source comes from one identifiable place; non-point source comes from many spread-out places.
2. What actually caused the Montara oil spill, and where?
A blowout at the West Atlas offshore drilling platform in the Timor Sea, north of Darwin.
3. Why can't agricultural run-off be "switched off" the way a single factory can?
It comes from many farms spread across an entire region, so there's no single source to target — it needs broad, coordinated change instead.

Chapter 12

The Ozone Layer

Sunscreen for the planet — and not the same problem as global warming.

High in the stratosphere, 15–35 km up, a thin band of ozone gas (O3) absorbs most of the sun's ultraviolet (UV) light — the part of sunlight that burns skin and can damage DNA. That's its entire job. It does not control temperature.

Key termTwo separate problems

Ozone depletion is caused by CFCs (once used in fridges and aerosols) destroying ozone. Global warming is caused by greenhouse gases trapping heat. Different gases, different mechanism, different fix — even though the two are constantly mixed up.

Case file

The Montreal Protocol (1987)

Scientists discovered CFCs were destroying the ozone layer, creating a hole over Antarctica. The Montreal Protocol banned these chemicals internationally — and the ozone layer is now recovering. It's one of the clearest examples of humanity successfully fixing an environmental problem once it was identified.

Field Test
1. What does the ozone layer actually filter out of sunlight?
Ultraviolet (UV) light, especially UV-B — not heat.
2. Why are ozone depletion and global warming two separate problems?
Ozone depletion is caused by CFCs damaging the ozone layer's ability to filter UV light; global warming is caused by greenhouse gases trapping heat that arrives as infrared radiation — different gases and mechanisms.
3. What did the Montreal Protocol do?
Signed in 1987, it banned CFCs and other ozone-depleting chemicals internationally, allowing the ozone layer to begin recovering.

Glossary of Key Terms

Abiotic factor — a non-living part of an ecosystem, such as sunlight, water or temperature.
Aquifer — an underground layer of rock or soil that stores water.
Arable land — land capable of being ploughed and used to grow seasonal crops.
Biodiversity — the variety of life, at the genetic, species and ecosystem levels.
Biome — a broad, major ecological zone, such as desert or rainforest.
Biotic factor — a living part of an ecosystem, such as a plant or animal.
Buffer zone — a protective strip of land between a human land use and a sensitive ecosystem.
Carbon footprint — a measure of greenhouse gas emissions caused by a person, product or activity.
Carrying capacity — the maximum population size an environment can support over time.
Day Zero — the point at which a reservoir has no water left to supply anyone.
Decomposer — an organism that breaks down dead material and recycles nutrients.
Density-dependent factor — a limiting factor that strengthens as a population gets more crowded.
Density-independent factor — a limiting factor that affects a population regardless of size.
Ecoregion — a specific region within a biome, defined by its own climate, land and life.
Ecosystem — a system in which organisms interact with each other and non-living conditions.
Ecosystem services — benefits people receive from nature: provisioning, regulating, supporting, cultural.
Erosion — the wearing away and movement of soil or rock by water, wind or ice.
Exponential growth — population growth that rises very quickly under ideal conditions.
Genetic diversity — variation in DNA within a single species.
Geosphere — the rocky, landform and soil part of the Earth.
Greenhouse gases — gases that absorb and re-emit heat energy in the atmosphere.
Hydrosphere — all of the water on Earth: oceans, lakes, rivers, groundwater, vapour.
Invasive species — a non-native organism that spreads and causes harm to a local ecosystem.
IUCN Red List — a formal global system classifying species by extinction risk.
Key Biodiversity Area — a site identified as especially important for species, habitats or ecological processes.
Leaching — the slow downward movement of water carrying dissolved nutrients through soil.
Logistic growth — population growth that levels off as it nears carrying capacity.
Monoculture — an ecosystem or farming system dominated by a single species.
Non-point source pollution — pollution from many spread-out sources, hard to trace and fix.
Non-renewable energy — an energy source used faster than it naturally forms, e.g. coal.
Ocean acidification — seawater becoming more acidic as it absorbs extra carbon dioxide.
Osmosis — water moving across a membrane from higher to lower concentration.
Ozone layer — a layer of ozone gas in the stratosphere that absorbs most UV light.
Point source pollution — pollution from one identifiable location.
Population — individuals of the same species living in a specific, named area at the same time.
Population density — how crowded individuals of a population are within a given area.
Precipitation — water falling from clouds as rain, snow or hail.
Primary forest — older, established forest with less recent human conversion.
Producer — an organism that makes its own food, forming the base of a food chain.
Renewable energy — an energy source naturally replenished on a human timescale.
Reservoir — a place where water is stored, often behind a dam.
Runoff — water flowing over the land into rivers and lakes.
Species diversity — the variety of different species present in an ecosystem.
The 10% energy rule — only around 10% of energy at one trophic level transfers to the next.
Transpiration — the release of water vapour from plants into the atmosphere.
Trophic level — a feeding position in a food chain — producer, primary consumer, and so on.

Exam Practice

Mixed questions across every chapter, in the style of a real end-of-course test. Try answering without looking back at the chapters, then open the answers below to check.

Section A — Short Answer (1–2 marks each)

  1. Define biodiversity.
  2. Name the three levels of biodiversity.
  3. What is the difference between a biotic factor and an abiotic factor?
  4. What is carrying capacity?
  5. Name Earth's four systems.
  6. What is the difference between point source and non-point source pollution?
  7. What is an aquifer?
  8. What is the difference between renewable and non-renewable energy?
  9. What does the ozone layer filter out of sunlight?
  10. What is a monoculture?

Section B — Extended Response (3–6 marks each)

  1. Explain the 10% energy rule and use it to explain why top predators are usually rare in an ecosystem. [4 marks]
  2. Compare exponential and logistic growth, and explain what causes a population to shift from one pattern to the other. [5 marks]
  3. Using a named example, explain how the IUCN Red List, Key Biodiversity Areas and AZE sites work together as a conservation-priority system. [6 marks]
  4. Describe the water cycle from evaporation to runoff, using all of: evaporation, condensation, precipitation, infiltration, aquifer. [5 marks]
  5. Explain why ozone depletion and global warming are two separate environmental problems. [4 marks]
  6. Using a real example, explain the difference between point source and non-point source pollution, and why one is generally easier to fix. [5 marks]

  1. Biodiversity is the variety of life, occurring at the genetic, species and ecosystem levels.
  2. Genetic diversity, species diversity, and ecosystem diversity.
  3. A biotic factor is a living part of an ecosystem; an abiotic factor is a non-living part.
  4. The maximum population size an environment can support over time.
  5. The geosphere, hydrosphere, atmosphere and biosphere.
  6. Point source pollution comes from one identifiable location; non-point source comes from many spread-out locations.
  7. An underground layer of rock or soil that stores water.
  8. Renewable energy is naturally replenished on a human timescale (e.g. solar); non-renewable forms over very long timescales and is used faster than replaced (e.g. coal).
  9. Ultraviolet (UV) light, especially UV-B.
  10. An ecosystem or farming system dominated by a single species.
  11. [Extended] Only around 10% of the energy at one trophic level is passed to the next, so each higher level supports far fewer individuals — this is why top predators are rare compared with producers.
  12. [Extended] Exponential growth is a rapidly rising J-curve under ideal conditions; logistic growth slows into an S-curve as density-dependent limiting factors (food, space) strengthen near carrying capacity.
  13. [Extended] The Red List classifies extinction risk; Key Biodiversity Areas flag globally important sites; AZE sites narrow this to the single most critical site for one highly threatened species (e.g. the Javan rhino) — together they direct limited conservation funding to where it matters most.
  14. [Extended] The sun evaporates water into vapour, which condenses into clouds, falls as precipitation, then either infiltrates into an aquifer or runs off back toward the sea.
  15. [Extended] Ozone depletion is caused by CFCs damaging the UV-filtering ozone layer; global warming is caused by greenhouse gases trapping infrared heat — different gases, different mechanisms, different fixes.
  16. [Extended] The Montara oil spill (a drilling-platform blowout) is point source, traceable to one rig and directly stoppable; Great Barrier Reef agricultural run-off is non-point source, spread across many farms, so it needs broad regional change rather than one fix.
